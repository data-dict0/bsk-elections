One term

This represents a single term of a barangay official in the Philippines.
Currently, a single term means four years in office.
But that has not been the case always. For decades, legislators have had a penchant for postponing barangay and Sangguniang Kabataan elections. Our tally puts at least 11 laws passed since 1994 that postponed village polls.
In almost all cases, postponing the elections meant public officials in office before laws were enacted stayed in holdover capacity until the winners had been proclaimed for the new election date.
That essentially amounts to a term extension. In year 2000, for instance, barangay officials whose terms were supposed to end stayed in office for an additional 27 months or over two years because Congress postponed the elections.
In 2016, terms of another set of barangay leaders were extended twice for a total of one year.
The current barangay leaders themselves have been governing in holdover capacity for nine months and an election is finally set for Nov. 2. Congress is working to delay that again.

Unlike terms for national posts such as the presidency or a seat in Congress, the terms of office for barangay and SK officials are not written in the Constitution but in specific laws. That makes it quite easy for lawmakers to alter them by simply passing a new law each time.
Since 1988, Congress has enacted laws that moved barangay and SK election dates between seven and 10 times, our calculations showed. Those postponements, in turn, resulted in local officials holding their posts for up to 42 months (3.5 years) longer than their required term.

Lawmakers have cited varying reasons each time they voted to postpone an election. The pandemic was one. In 2002, Congress also wanted to synchronize the barangay and SK polls for efficiency after years of holding them separately. "These are valid reasons. But cutting costs is not. If it was, then why would we even conduct elections every three years?" Michael Henry Yusingco, a senior research fellow at Ateneo Policy Center, said.
This time, lawmakers are saying it will be too expensive to conduct an election with high inflation raising the prices of basic commodities. But the same Congress already allocated additional P9 billion in funding to the Commission on Elections (Comelec) last year precisely to fund the upcoming polls. Granted, the higher budget was meant to cover salaries of teachers as election staff and not to cover other expenses, but Comelec has also not indicated it is short of funds. On the contrary, the agency has said it is on track on its election preparations.
Not only have legislators repeatedly tinkered with barangay and SK election schedules, they have also revised the terms of office for these local officials numerous times. A bill already approved on second reading at the Senate is just another example of this: the proposed law would supposedly fix barangay and SK leaders' terms of office to five years, up from the current four years which was set just last year by another law.

In practice, it seems terms are not really fixed, anyway. Barangay officers have often governed longer than mandated because elections were delayed and they were placed in holdover capacity. In fact, since 1995, only three sets of barangay and SK officials actually served their full term and left as scheduled by the law.
"Lawmakers seem to be playing with their rights. It also downplays their positions," Jean Franco, a political scientist at University of the Philippines, said. "This looks like they see SK and barangay leaders as mere pawns rather than agents of change and governance in the grassroots."
In the end, the voters are at the losing end of election postponements, Yusingco said. Admittedly, barangays and the SK barely receive the public attention that national positions do, but these small localities are often the first in extending immediate help to the public. For instance, at the height of the pandemic lockdowns, barangays were tapped to quickly locate and distribute cash aid to beneficiaries.
The good news is majority of Filipino voters remain enthusiastic to vote for their new leaders, but recent delays appear to dampen the mood. This is obvious specifically in the SK where voter turnout has historically been higher than that in presidential and midterm elections before 2018. That year, SK elections were held after a four-year delay.

Voter turnout did recover on the last barangay and SK elections in 2023, but it remains to be seen whether that would continue given the planned impending delay. In 2023, the Supreme Court already ruled that postponing barangay and SK elections is unconstitutional without a "compelling" and "substantial" reason. The court specified a criteria that makes election delays acceptable, including a "public emergency."
Despite that ruling, Congress still enacted another law that delayed the barangay and SK elections scheduled last year to Nov. 2026 and now plans to push back that date further.
"The only way is through public pressure... If Filipinos see the barangay as a forum where they can influence governance, then they would likely fight any attempt by lawmakers to postpone the election," Yusingco, a lawyer, said.
"But the public is generally apathetic to this matter because they don't know how valuable the barangay is to improve politics and governance. And the reality is, only the people can stop lawmakers from disobeying the [Supreme Court] in this regard," he added. Prinz Magtulis
